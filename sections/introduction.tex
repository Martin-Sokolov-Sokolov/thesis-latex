\section{Introduction}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Introduce the problem FVS in the most general setting (important problem, many ways to solve it)
    \item Mention approximation algorithms as a way to deal with the NP-hardness of the problem 
    \item Baker's technique as a means of acquiring a PTAS
    \item  Other means of acquiring an approximation ratio, specifically a constant-factor approximation, LP
    \item BWCA, certified algorithms
\end{itemize}

\noindent The \textsc{Feedback Vertex Set} (\textsc{FVS}) problem is central to the field of computer science. In Karp's paper \cite{Karp1972} 
it was shown that there is a reduction from \textsc{Satisfiability} to twenty one problems, one of which is \textsc{FVS}. Because of the 
hardness of the problem, extensive research has been conducted in order to find a good approximation solution. The problem is known to be \textbf{APX-complete} 
and the best approximation ratio so far is two and was first found by Becker and Geiger \cite{BECKER1996167}. Around the same time, Bafna et al. 
\cite{bafnaandfujito} devised the first local ratio technique in order to arrive at the same approximation ratio. These algorithms were translated 
in primal-dual terms by Chudak et al. \cite{CHUDAK1998111} who devise a third algorithm which yield a 2-approximation both for the unweighted and 
weighted case of the \textsc{FVS} problem. 

\noindent For planar graphs and \textsc{H-Minor-Free} graphs two approaches have yielded PTASs for many problems. Baker's technique \cite{Baker} 
focused on approximation algorithms for \textbf{NP-Complete} problems on planar graphs while Demaine and Hajiaghayi \cite{demaine2005bidimensionality} 
generalized the shifting technique for the latter class of graphs. One of the known limitations of the shifting technique is that it does not
handle non-local problems such as the ones we are examining (\textsc{FVS}, \textsc{Cycle Packing}). In our paper we impose restrictions on 
the problems in order to introduce a local property that allows us to devise a PTAS for the \textsc{FVS-BCL} problem.
Baker's technique allows for the translation into the weighted version of the problem. We adapt the algorithm to work for the \textsc{MWFVS-BCL} 
all the while trying to follow the framework for devising certified algorithms established in 
\cite{Makarychev_Makarychev_2021, angelidakis2018bilu,bumpus_et_al:LIPIcs.SWAT.2024.19}.

\noindent The \textsc{FVS} problem has applications in the field of Bayesian networks. As we said, researchers turned to approximation algorithms 
and made use of the linear programming framework in order to get desirable ratios. In this paper, we make use of the existing literature on
\textsc{LP} for \textsc{FVS} and analyse how to translate the results for the class of graphs with bounded cycle length. In Section \ref{BWCA-section} 
we familiarize the reader with two ways of designing certified algorithms. One of the ways is by using convex relations. This motivates us to 
define the problem as an \textsc{ILP}, solve the \textsc{LP} relaxation and exploit a randomized rounding scheme to get the values for the 
approximate and co-approximate conditions, as defined in the same section. Those values are then used to calculate the $\gamma$ for the 
$\gamma-$certified algorithm.

\noindent Finally, we consider the notions of Bilu-Linial stability \cite{bilu2012stable} and certified algorithms \cite{Makarychev_Makarychev_2021}. 
In the last two paragraphs we explained how we plan to translate existing techniques to get certified solutions of instances. The last sections 
will be dedicated to proving that these algorithms are indeed certified. 

\noindent \textbf{Related Work}

This work is inspired by Baker's seminal paper on designing PTASs in planar graphs \cite{Baker}. There have been other techniques that 
generalize this approach, namely Demaine and Hajiaghayi \cite{demaine2005bidimensionality}'s bidimensionality theory. The latter framework 
explores problems in \textsc{H-Minor-Free} graphs. 

\noindent The notion of Bilu-Linial stability has given rise to much exploration of structured instances in many problems. 
In their paper Bilu and Linial \cite{bilu2012stable} offer the first evidence that stable instances are much easier than worst-case instances of problems. 
They propose an exact algorithm for $O(n)$-stable instances of \textsc{Max Cut}. This result was improved by Bilu et al. \cite{bilu2012practically} 
who designed a $O(\sqrt{n})$ for the same problem. Further research yielded a general approach to analysing stable instances in 
Makarychev et al. \cite{makarychev2014bilu}. They applied that approach to graph partitioning problems and designed 
$O(\sqrt{\log n}\log\log n)$-stable instances of \textsc{Max Cut} and 4-stable instances of \textsc{Minimum Multiway Cut}. The study of
perturbation resilience of clustering problems is explored in \cite{awasthi2012center, balcan2016clustering, balcan2015k}.
On the \textsc{Travelling Salesman Problem}, Mihal\'ak et al. \cite{mihalak2011complexity} gave an algorithm for 1.8-stable instances. 
We refer the reader to the survey \cite{makarychev201713}.

\noindent On the subject of Beyond the Worst-Case Analysis we refer to Makarychev and Makarychev \cite{Makarychev_Makarychev_2021}. 
In that paper certified algorithms are first described in an attempt to bridge the gap between the worst-case and structured instances. 
Since then, researchers have made use of the definitions and results in that paper and applied them to other problems.
Angelidakis et al. \cite{angelidakis2018bilu} explore the notion of stability and initiate the study of certified algorithms 
for the \textsc{Maximum Independent Set} problem (\textsc{MIS}). They provide robust and certified algorithms for $(1 + \epsilon)$-stable instances 
of planar \textsc{MIS}, for any fixed $\epsilon > 0$. Covering and Dominating in planar graphs was further explored by Bumpus et al. \cite{bumpus_et_al:LIPIcs.SWAT.2024.19}. 
They provide ($1 + \epsilon$)-certified algorithms for \textsc{Dominating Set} and \textsc{H-Subgraph-Free-Deletion}, which for any $\epsilon$ 
run in time $f(1/\epsilon) \times n^{O(1)}$ on minor-closed classes of graphs of bounded local treewidth.
