\section{(E)PTAS for the FVS-BCL Problem Using Baker's Technique}

\noindent In this section we will demonstrate how to obtain an EPTAS for the
unweighted and weighted versions of FVS-BCL.
We begin 
by looking at the unweighted version and argue about the correctness and efficiency of our algorithm. 
Then we prove that using treewidth and Monadic Second Order Logic (MSOL) one can exactly solve the 
4-cycle-breaking problem in one subgraph.
Finally, we show that the running time is indeed polynomial in 
the size of the input.

\noindent Before we start, we introduce some notation that we are going to use from now on. 
When we are looking to break 4-cycles, we will note that the problem as FVS-4CL. This will be the 
notation used in subsections~\ref{subsec:baker_unweighted_fvs} and \ref{subsec:baker_weighted_fvs}, 
i. e. regardless of whether or not the problem is weighted. In subsection~\ref{subsec:weighted_arbitrary_length} 
we go back to calling the problem $\Pi$ - FVS-BCL as we have arbitrary cycle length $\rho$. 
In further chapters we also make use of that notation and if mentioned explicitly,
one should regard FVS-4CL as the weighted version as this work is ultimately interested
in that version.

\subsection{Baker's technique for unweighted FVS-4CL on planar graphs}
\label{subsec:baker_unweighted_fvs}

\noindent In this subsection we provide the necessary definitions, lemmas and theorems to demonstrate how to obtain an EPTAS for the problem 
we defined in \ref{FVS-BCL}, namely FVS-4CL. In the preliminaries we gave two examples of how to construct algorithms using Baker's technique 
and how to further analyse them. In the same fashion we now construct Algorithm \ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} and reason for its effectiveness.

\noindent In the following algorithm we use the letter $\ell$ for the outerplanarity of each subgraph, i.e. each $G_{i, j}$ that we 
define on line~\ref{alg3:line4} in Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} is $\ell$-outerplanar.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{Baker's technique for the unweighted FVS-4CL}
    \label{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Planar graph $G = (V, E)$, parameter $\ell$
    \ENSURE $(1 + \epsilon)$-approximation for \textsc{FVS-4CL}
    \STATE Perform \textsc{BFS} from some arbitrary vertex $r$ \label{alg3:line1}
    \STATE $S \leftarrow \emptyset$

    $i$: \textit{shift}; $j$: \textit{slice}

    \FOR{each $i = 0$ to $\ell-1$} \label{alg3:for-loop}
        \STATE Let $G_{i, j}$ be the subgraph induced on vertices at levels
        $j \cdot \ell + i$ through $(j+1) \cdot \ell + i + 1$ for $0 \leq i < \ell$. \label{alg3:line4}
        \STATE $\forall j \text{ compute }  S_{i, j}$, the min. unweighted \textsc{FVS-4CL} 
        of $G_{i, j}$ using Algorithm~\ref{courcelle:unweighted} as a subroutine. \label{alg3:subroutine}
        \STATE $\forall j \text{ let } S_i = \bigcup_{j} S_{i, j}$ \label{alg3:line6}
        \STATE $S \leftarrow S \cup S_i$
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $S_{i*}$ from $S$ with minimum cardinality \label{alg3:line9}
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent In the analysis of the algorithm we are going to need three lemmas in order 
to prove that Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} obtains a $(1 + 2/\ell)$-approximation of the 
optimum solution.

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Optimum solution bound (Unweighted Case)]{Lemma}
    \label{lemma-one}

    Let $S_{i, j}$ be an optimum solution for $G_{i, j}$ for some shift $i$ and slice $j$, 
    let $F \equiv OPT$ be the optimum solution in $G$ and let $F_{i, j} = G_{i, j} \cap F$. 
    In the unweighted case we claim that: \[|S_{i, j}| \leq |F_{i, j}|\text{.}\]

    We will say that $F_{i, j}$ is a FVS-4CL of $G_{i, j}$, not necessarily the one of minimum cardinality.

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent For now we do not argue about how we obtain an optimum solution.
In subsection~\ref{subsection:exact-alg} we show how to get an exact solution, but for now we argue
that the inequality in Lemma~\ref{lemma-one} holds in each subgraph $G_{i, j}$.

\noindent \textit{Proof.}
We first argue that in each subgraph $G_{i, j}$, the set $F_{i, j}$ contains a vertex that 
breaks every 4-cycle.  Let us fix a slice and shift $a$ and $b$ and take a look at the subgraph $G_{a, b}$, 
the optimal solution for it $S_{a, b}$ and the set $F_{a, b}$. Notice that, much like in the Vertex Cover
analysis, no vertex in $V(G) \backslash V(G_{a, b})$ can break any 4-cycle that is contained within 
$G_{a, b}$. Therefore $F_{a, b}$ must contain a vertex that hits every 4-cycle or it will not be 
a solution in $G_{a, b}$ and subsequently in $G$. From this and the fact that $S_{a, b}$ is the optimum 
solution for $G_{a, b}$ we conclude that: \[|S_{a, b}| \leq |F_{a, b}|\text{.}\]
for some $(a, b)$, therefore it holds $\forall (i, j)$. 

\noindent One must confront the fact that notation is abused throughout the paper, especially when it 
comes down to $j$, the slice variable. When we say that a statement holds for all values $i$ and
$j$, we mean that it holds for the subgraphs that these variables outline. We will sketch an example 
to deepen our understanding of Lemma~\ref{lemma-one}. Moreover, we are going to explore one avenue 
that gives rise to an inequality in the lemma.

\noindent Let $G$ look like the graph we sketched on the previous page. The vertical dots help us 
clarify the levels while the horizontal signify that there may be other vertices of interest. However, 
there are vertices we would like to inspect, specifically vertices with labels $14$ and $17$. Let 
$v_{14}, v_{17} \in F$. What follows is that $v_{14}, v_{17} \in F_{0, 0}$. The cycles that give us
reason to claim that $v_{14} \in F$ are all within $G_{0, 0}$. Therefore, it holds that
Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} will have $v_{14} \in S_{0, 0}$. However, the same does not hold for 
$v_{17}$, the cycles that it is a part of are in $G_{0, 1}$ and thus $v_{17} \notin S_{0, 0}$, while 
$v_{17} \in F_{0, 0}$. This example should not distract the reader from the fact the real reason 
that $|S_{0, 0}| \leq |F_{0, 0}|$ is because $S_{0, 0}$ is by definition the minimum 4-cycle-breaking 
set for $G_{0, 0}$, while $F_{0, 0}$ is, as we previously argued, a feasible 4-cycle-breaking set.

\noindent What follows from Lemma~\ref{lemma-one} is a result that holds for any shift $i$.

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Optimum solution bound for the whole graph (Unweighted Case)]{Result}
    \label{result-two}

    Let $S_i$ be the union of optimal solutions defined on line~\ref{alg3:line6} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} 
    for some shift $i$. 
    Let $F \equiv OPT$ be the optimum solution in $G$ and let $F_{i, j} = G_{i, j} \cap F$.
    For those sets it holds that: \[|S_{i}| \leq \sum\limits_{j} |S_{i, j}| \leq \sum\limits_{j} |F_{i, j}|\text{.}\]  

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent The first inequality in Result~\ref{result-two} stems from the fact that some vertices 
in the $S_{i, j}$ sum may appear twice. This pertains to vertices that are located on the boundary. 
For example if the $v_{30} \in S_{0, 1} \wedge v_{30} \in S_{0, 2}$, then it would be counted once in 
$S_0$, but twice in $\sum\limits_j S_{0,j}$.
The second inequality is a result from Lemma~\ref{lemma-one} 
where we argue that $S_{i, j}$ is an optimum solution for $G_{i, j}$ while $F_{i, j}$ is a 
4-cycle-breaking set, but not one of necessarily minimum cardinality. 

\noindent In Lemma~\ref{lemma-three} we look at the bound of the union of the $F_{i, j}$ sets. 
Before that, however, we make an argument for the vertices on the boundaries in Lemma~\ref{lemma-two}. 

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Bound for the vertices on the boundaries (Unweighted Case)]{Lemma}
    \label{lemma-two}

    Let $OPT_i = OPT \cap$ \{vertices at levels $i$ mod $\ell\}$. 
    The sets $OPT_i$ are disjoint and $\bigcup_i OPT_i = OPT$.

    We claim that: \[\exists q \in \{0, 1, \dots, \ell-1\}:
    |OPT_q| + |OPT_{q+1}| \leq \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot |OPT| \text{.}\]

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent \textit{Proof.}
Assume, by way of contradiction, that:

\begin{equation}
    \forall q: |OPT_q| + |OPT_{q+1}| > \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot |OPT|
    \label{ineq:opt_bound}
\end{equation}

\noindent As we said those sets are disjoint, meaning $OPT_q \cap OPT_{q+1} = \emptyset$. We make the following arguments:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item If inequality \ref{ineq:opt_bound} holds,
    then $\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1} |OPT_i| + |OPT_{i+1}| > 2 \cdot |OPT|$. 
    \item Because each $OPT_i$ exists twice in that sum and 
    $OPT_i \cap OPT_j = \emptyset$, $\forall (i, j), i \neq j$, then 
    the property $\bigcup_i OPT_i = OPT$ from which follows $\sum\limits_{i}^{\ell-1} |OPT_i| = |OPT|$
    would not hold.
    \item From the contradiction in step two, we argue that there indeed 
    $\exists q: q \in \{0, 1, \dots, \ell-1\}$ by the \textit{pigeonhole principle},
    such that $|OPT_q| + |OPT_{q+1}| \leq \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot |OPT|$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Now we argue for the size of the sets $F_{i, j}$. It is natural that we should be able 
to tie their size to the size of the optimal solution $F$. We will use Lemma~\ref{lemma-two} to do 
so. First we will state the lemma.

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Bound for the cardinality of the intersection sets (Unweighted Case)]{Lemma}
    \label{lemma-three}

    Let $F \equiv OPT$ be the optimal solution and $F_{i, j} = F \cap G_{i, j}$ be the intersection 
    sets with the subgraphs. Then we have: 
    \[\sum\limits_{j} |F_{i, j}| \leq |F| + \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot |F|\]

    for some specific integer $i$.

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent \textit{Proof.}
If we look at Fig.~\ref{fig:bfs_tree}, then we notice that any vertex that is located 
in the overlap layers would be counted twice in the sum $\sum\limits_{j} |F_{i, j}|$. The interior 
vertices are counted once towards the optimum solution sum. Therefore, we have that: 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\sum\limits_{j} |F_{i, j}| = |F| + |\{\text{vertices on the boundary}\}| = |F| + |OPT_i| + |OPT_{i+1}|$
    for some $i \in \{0, 1, \dots, \ell-1\}$. \label{enum:eq1}
    \item For the RHS of the equation in step~\ref{enum:eq1}. we argue that:
    \[\exists i^* |OPT_{i^*}| + |OPT_{i^*+1}| \leq \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot |OPT|\] using Lemma~\ref{lemma-two}.
    \item For that $i^*$ it holds that $\sum\limits_{j} |F_{i^*, j}| \leq |F| + \frac{2}{\ell} |F|$.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, we chain the inequalities presented in Lemma~\ref{lemma-one}, Result~\ref{result-two}
and Lemma~\ref{lemma-three}, and get that for some slice $i^*$, it holds that:

\[|S_{i^*}| \leq \sum\limits_{j} |S_{i^*, j}| \leq \sum\limits_{j} |F_{i^*, j}| \leq |F| + \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot |F| = (1 + \frac{2}{\ell}) \cdot |F|\].

\noindent The inequality $S_{i^*} \leq (1 + \frac{2}{\ell}) \cdot |F|$, gives us that the solution 
produced by Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} has the approximation ratio $(1 + \frac{2}{\ell})$ for the planar 
unweighted FVS-4CL.

\noindent \textbf{Running time:}

\noindent Lines~\ref{alg3:line1} and~\ref{alg3:line9} of Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} 
can be computed in linear time $O(n)$. The loop that starts on line~\ref{alg3:for-loop} has a running time
that is dominated by step~\ref{alg3:subroutine} where we call Algorithm~\ref{courcelle:unweighted} as a subroutine.
That algorithm takes as input subgraphs that are $\ell + 2$-outerplanar. Using a result from 
Bodlaender~\cite[Theorem 83]{bodlaender1998partial}, we get that the treewidth of each of those 
subgraphs is $3\cdot\ell+6-1=3\cdot\ell+5$. Finally, using Theorem~\ref{theorem:courcelle}, 
we conclude, that the for loop takes $O(f(3\ell+5)\cdot\ell\cdot n)$ for some computable function $f$. Therefore,
Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} has a running time of 
$O(n) + O(f(3\ell + 5)\cdot\ell\cdot n) + O(n) = O(f(\ell)\cdot\ell\cdot n)$.

\subsection{Baker's technique for the weighted FVS-4CL on planar graphs}
\label{subsec:baker_weighted_fvs}

\noindent In this subsection we look at the weighted version of the problem. 
Firstly, we construct Algorithm~\ref{alg:weighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} which differs from Algorithm~\ref{alg:unweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} 
only on step~\ref{alg4:step5}. On line~\ref{alg4:step5}
we construct Algorithm~\ref{courcelle:weighted} and use it as a subroutine to get the solution we want.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{Baker's technique for the weighted FVS-4CL}
    \label{alg:weighted_feedback_vertex_set_global}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Planar graph $G = (V, E)$, parameter $\ell$, $w : V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$
    \ENSURE $(1 + \epsilon)$-approximation for \textsc{FVS-4CL}
    \STATE Perform \textsc{BFS} from some arbitrary vertex $r$
    \STATE $S \leftarrow \emptyset$

    $i$: \textit{shift}; $j$: \textit{slice}

    \FOR{each $i = 0$ to $\ell-1$}
        \STATE Let $G_{i, j}$ be the subgraph induced on vertices at levels $j \cdot \ell + i$ through $(j+1) \cdot \ell + i + 1$ for $0 \leq i < \ell$.
        \STATE $\forall j \text{ compute }  S_{i, j}$, the min. weighted \textsc{FVS-4CL} 
        of $G_{i, j}$ using Algorithm~\ref{courcelle:weighted} as a subroutine. \label{alg4:step5}
        \STATE $\forall j \text{ let } S_i = \bigcup_{j} S_{i, j}$
        \STATE $S \leftarrow S \cup S_i$
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $S_{i*}$ from $S$, such that $w(S_{i^*}) \leq w(S_{i}) , \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, \ell-1\}$.
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent Much like the proof we did in subsection~\ref{subsec:baker_unweighted_fvs}, we start off with 
finding a bound for the optimum solution for each subgraph. 

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Optimum solution bound (Weighted Case)]{Lemma}
    \label{lemma-osbwc}

    Let $G = (V, E)$ be a planar graph and $w : V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a weight function.
    Let $S_{i, j}$ be an optimum solution for $G_{i, j}$ for some shift $i$ and slice $j$, 
    let $F \equiv OPT$ be the optimum solution in $G$ and let $F_{i, j} = G_{i, j} \cap F$. 
    In the weighted case we claim that: \[w(S_{i, j}) \leq w(F_{i, j})\text{.}\]

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent \textit{Proof.}
The proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma-osbwc} is presented in three steps. Let us fix the integer arguments for 
shift and slice. We take $a$ to be the offset (shift) and look at an arbitrary slice $b$. Note that 
when we look at a slice $b$, we mean that we look at the subgraph $G_{a, b}$ induced on vertices at levels 
$b \cdot \ell + a$ through $(b + 1) \cdot \ell + a + 1$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Firstly, one needs to be sure that no vertex $v$, such that $v \in V(G) \backslash V(G_{a, b})$ 
    can break any 4-cycle that is contained within $G_{a, b}$.
    \item From step 1 and the definition of $F_{a, b}$, it follows that $F_{a, b}$ is a feasible solution 
    of the 4-cycle-breaking problem within $G_{a, b}$.
    \item Finally we argue, that because $S_{a, b}$ is an optimum solution for the 4-cycle-breaking problem 
    within $G_{a, b}$ and $F_{a, b}$ is a feasible solution for the same problem, we have: 
    \[w(S_{a, b}) \leq w(F_{a, b})\].
\end{enumerate}

The statement in Lemma~\ref{lemma-osbwc} holds for all values for the shift and slice. Now we mention 
a useful result that follows from the lemma.

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Optimum solution bound for the whole graph (Weighted Case)]{Result}
    \label{result-osbwc}

    Let $S_i$ be the union of optimal solutions defined on line 6 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:weighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} 
    for some shift $i$.
    Let $F \equiv OPT$ be the optimum solution in $G$ and let $F_{i, j} = G_{i, j} \cap F$.
    For those sets it holds that: \[w(S_{i}) \leq \sum\limits_{j} w(S_{i, j}) \leq \sum\limits_{j} w(F_{i, j}) \text{.}\]  

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent \textit{Proof.} This result immediately follows from the fact that when we define $S_i$ as the union of the subgraphs 
some vertices, specifically those on the overlapping layers, will have their weights added twice 
to the sum $\sum\limits_{j} w(S_{i, j})$. If we inspect the LHS, however, each vertex and its weight will be 
counted only once towards $w(S_i)$. Therefore, it holds that $w(S_i) \leq \sum\limits_{j} w(S_{i, j})$ 
and from Lemma~\ref{lemma-osbwc} we chain the inequalities to get Result~\ref{result-osbwc}.

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Bound for the weight of the vertices on the boundary (Weighted Case)]{Lemma}
    \label{lemma-bvweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global}

    Let $G = (V, E)$ be a planar graph and suppose we run a BFS from an arbitrary vertex.
    Let $w : V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a weight function.
    Let $OPT_i = OPT \cap$ \{vertices at levels $i$ mod $\ell\}$. 
    The sets $OPT_i$ are disjoint and $\bigcup_i OPT_i = OPT$.

    We claim that: \[\exists q \in \{0, 1, \dots, \ell-1\}:
    w(OPT_{q}) + w(OPT_{q+1}) \leq \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(OPT) \text{.}\]

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent \textit{Proof.} Assume, by way of contradiction, that:

\begin{equation}
    \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, \ell-1\}, \quad w(OPT_i) + w(OPT_{i+1}) > \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(OPT).
    \label{ineq:two}
\end{equation}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item From the two properties in the definition of $OPT_i$, namely that
    
    $OPT_i \cap OPT_j = \emptyset, \forall i, j \in \{1, 2, \dots, \ell-1\}, i \neq j$ and 

    $OPT = \bigcup_i OPT_i$, it follows that $\sum\limits_{i = 0}^{\ell-1} w(OPT_i) = w(OPT)$.
    
    \item However, if inequality \ref{ineq:two} holds,
    then $\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1} w(OPT_i) + w(OPT_{i+1}) > 
    \sum\limits_{i=0}^{\ell-1} \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(OPT) >
    2 \cdot w(OPT)$. 
    Because each $OPT_i$ is counted twice in the sum of step 2, then there is a contradiction between 
    the sums in steps 1 and 2. 

    \item Finally, we claim that because of the \textit{pigeonhole principle} there must exist a 
    "small pair" $OPT_q$ and $OPT_{q+1}$, such that $w(OPT_q) + w(OPT_{q+1}) \leq \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(OPT)$.

\end{enumerate}

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Bound for the weight of the intersection sets (Weighted Case)]{Lemma}
    \label{lemma-bcisweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global}

    Let $F \equiv OPT$ be the optimal solution and $F_{i, j} = F \cap G_{i, j}$ be the intersection 
    sets with the subgraphs. Then we have: 
    \[\sum\limits_{j} w(F_{i, j}) \leq w(F) + \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(F)\]

    for some specific integer $i$.

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent The interior vertices of each subgraph have their weights counted only once towards the sum 
$\sum\limits_j w(F_{i, j})$. The same does not hold for the vertices on the boundary, they are counted 
once in $F_{i, j}$ and once in $F_{i, j+1}$. To account for that we rewrite the sum on the LHS as:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\sum\limits_j w(F_{i, j}) = w(F) + w(\{\text{vertices on the boundary}\})$.
    \item Now we can rewrite the RHS of 1 as:
    
    $w(F) + w(\{\text{vertices on the boundary}\}) = w(F) + w(OPT_i) + w(OPT_{i+1})$ for some 
    $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, \ell-1\}$.
    \item From Lemma~\ref{lemma-bvweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} we have an integer $i^* \in \{1, 2, \dots, \ell-1\}$, such that:
    
    $w(OPT_{i^*}) + w(OPT_{i^* + 1}) \leq \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(F)$.
    \item For the integer $i^*$ in step 3 we substitute in step 2 and get:
    
    $w(F) + w(OPT_{i^*}) + w(OPT_{i^* + 1}) \leq w(F) + \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(F)$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Finally, we chain the inequalities we obtained in Lemma~\ref{lemma-osbwc}, Result~\ref{result-osbwc}, 
and Lemma~\ref{lemma-bcisweighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} to argue that for some $i^* \in \{1, 2, \dots, \ell-1\}$: 

\[w(S_{i^*}) \leq \sum\limits_j w(S_{i^*, j}) \leq \sum\limits_j w(F_{i^*, j}) \leq w(F) + \frac{2}{\ell} \cdot w(F)\].

\noindent \textbf{Running time:}

\noindent The only difference from the unweighted case comes from line~\ref{alg4:step5} of 
Algorithm~\ref{alg:weighted_feedback_vertex_set_global}. We call a different subroutine, 
namely Algorithm~\ref{courcelle:weighted}. However, using Theorem~\ref{theorem:courcelle}, 
we get that the running time of the algorithm is $O(f(\ell)\cdot\ell\cdot n)$ for some 
computable function $f$.

\subsection{Baker's technique for weighted FVS-BCL of arbitrary cycle length}
\label{subsec:weighted_arbitrary_length}

\noindent In this subsection we generalize what we saw in subsection~\ref{subsec:baker_weighted_fvs} and inspect 
what happens when we try to break all cycles of length $\rho$. To reiterate, our algorithm is only 
interested in breaking cycles of length $\rho$. 

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{Baker's technique for the weighted FVS-BCL}
    \label{alg:baker_arbitrary_length}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Planar graph $G = (V, E)$, parameter $\ell \leftarrow \frac{1}{\epsilon}$, $w : V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, 
    $\rho$ - integer specifying cycles of what length to break
    \ENSURE $(1 + \epsilon)$-approximation for \textsc{FVS-BCL}
    \STATE Perform \textsc{BFS} from some arbitrary vertex $r$
    \STATE $S \leftarrow \emptyset$

    $i$: \textit{shift}; $j$: \textit{slice}

    \FOR{each $i = 0$ to $\ell-1$}
        \STATE Let $G_{i, j}$ be the subgraph induced on vertices at levels 
        $j \cdot \ell + i$ through 
        $(j+1) \cdot \ell + i + \lfloor \frac{\rho}{2} \rfloor$ 
        for $0 \leq i < \ell$.
        \STATE $\forall j \text{ compute }  S_{i, j}$, the min. weighted \textsc{FVS-BCL} 
        of $G_{i, j}$ using Algorithm~\ref{courcelle:rho} as a subroutine.
        \STATE $\forall j \text{ let } S_i = \bigcup_{j} S_{i, j}$
        \STATE $S \leftarrow S \cup S_i$
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $S_{i*}$ from $S$, such that $w(S_{i^*}) \leq w(S_{i}) , \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, \ell-1\}$.
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent The changes between Algorithm~\ref{alg:weighted_feedback_vertex_set_global} and 
Algorithm~\ref{alg:baker_arbitrary_length} pertain to lines 4-7. The main difference is on line 4 where 
we define the subgraphs we want to inspect. This change enables us to argue that the union 
of the local solutions, defined on line 6, is indeed a feasible solution for the whole graph. 
What we then have to prove is that this solution is not only feasible, but for some integer $i^*$, 
it is optimal.

\noindent In Lemma~\ref{lemma:optimum_solution_bound_rho_cycle_length} we present the two results 
we got in Lemma~\ref{lemma-osbwc} and Result~\ref{result-osbwc} for arbitrary cycle length.

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Optimum solution bound for FVS-$\rho$CL]{Lemma}
    \label{lemma:optimum_solution_bound_rho_cycle_length}

    Let $G = (V, E)$ be a planar graph, $w : V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be a weight function and 
    $\rho$ an integer. Let $F \equiv OPT$ be the optimum solution in the whole graph for 
    FVS-$\rho$-BCL and $F_{i, j} = G_{i, j} \cap F$.
    Let $S_{i, j}$ be an optimum solution for $G_{i, j}$ for some shift $i$ and slice $j$.
    We claim that: \[w(S_i) \leq \sum\limits_{j} w(S_{i, j}) \leq \sum\limits_{j} w(F_{i, j})\text{.}\]

\end{mydefinition}

\noindent We first prove the second inequality by choosing random shift and slice variables and seeing that the 
inequality holds. In this step we deviate from Lemma~\ref{lemma-osbwc} in that when we choose $(a, b)$ 
to be the aforementioned variables, we inspect the induced subgraph $G_{a, b}$ on levels 
$b \cdot \ell + a$ through $(b + 1) \cdot \ell + a + \lfloor \frac{\rho}{2} \rfloor$. In that subgraph,
the optimum solution $S_{a, b}$ found on step 5 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:baker_arbitrary_length} is better than the 
feasible solution $F_{a, b}$ for $G_{a, b}$.

\noindent The first inequality holds, because the vertices on the boundaries are counted twice towards 
the RHS of the inequality $w(S_i) \leq \sum\limits_{j} w(S_{i, j})$.




\subsection{Exact algorithm for FVS-4CL}
\label{subsection:exact-alg}

\noindent In this subsection we are looking for an algorithm that finds exact solutions to the 
FVS-4CL problem in each subgraph. For each subgraph we can find the treewidth. 

\noindent We take a logical approach that was introduced by Courcelle~\cite{courcelle1990monadic}. 
He shows that graph properties, that can be stated in monadic second-order logic (MSOL), can be solved in 
linear time for graphs of bounded treewidth. In the following two subsections we briefly explain MSOL and 
then apply it to our problem. We follow a survey on the matter in Satzinger \cite{satzinger2014tree}. 

\subsubsection{MSOL - a language for graph problems}

\noindent We want to introduce a language that translates graph problems to formulas, therefore reducing 
the problem to a satisfiability problem. Consequently, our goal is to find a formula $\Phi$ with the 
property: 

\[\text{The problem $\Pi$ is solvable for a graph $G$ \textit{iff} $G \models \Phi$, i. e. the formula 
$\Phi$ is satisfied by the graph $G$.}\]

\noindent Problem $\Pi$ is solvable in polynomial (in this case linear) time for graphs of treewidth at 
most $k$.

\noindent Monadic second-order logic is an extension of first-order logic. Additionally to having 
object variables $x, y \dots$, logical connectives $\lnot, \land, \lor, \lnot,
\rightarrow, \longleftrightarrow$ and quantifiers for object variables $\forall x, 
\exists x$, MSOL considers sets of object variables $X, Y \dots$, and relation to them 
via $\in$.

\noindent Whenever graph problems are involved, typically we express solution through 
vertices and edges. Additionally, as we mentioned, we can construct sets of vertices 
and edges. Therefore we have the following machinery:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{•}]
    \item Object variables: \textit{vertices}: $v_1, v_2, \dots$ and \textit{edges}: $e_1, e_2, \dots$. 
    \item Set variables: sets of vertices $V_1, V_2, \dots$ and sets of edges $E_1, E_2, \dots$.
    \item Binary relation $\in : \{\text{object variable}\} \times \{\text{set variable}\} \to \{0, 1\}$. Therefore 
    $v \in V$ \textit{iff} $v$ is an element of the corresponding set $V$.
    \item The $Adj(e, v_i, v_j)$ relation. It detects whether edge $e$ is an edge from vertex $v_i$ to 
    vertex $v_j$ where $v_i \neq v_j$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent With this machinery, one obtains a first-order language suitable for expressing relations in a graph. 
To extend this to a traditional second-order logic language, one must add quantifications over set 
variables. 

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{•}]
    \item Quantification over set variables: $\forall V_i, \forall E_i$ and $\exists V_i, \exists E_i$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Exact algorithm for unweighted FVS-4CL using MSOL}

\noindent Courcelle's work was extended to extended monadic second-order logic 
\cite{arnborg1991easy,borie1991deterministic,courcelle1993monadic}. These extensions allow to optimize 
over the size and weight of a free set variable.

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \label{courcelle:unweighted}
    \min_{F \subseteq V} & \ |F|: \\
    & \land \forall v u w z: \\
    & v \in (V \backslash F) \land u \in (V \backslash F) \land w \in (V \backslash F) \land z \in (V \backslash F) \\
    & \land v \neq w \land u \neq z \\
    & \land (v, u) \in E \land (u, w) \in E \land (w, z) \in E \land (v, z) \in E \to \bot
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsubsection{Exact algorithm for weighted FVS-4CL using MSOL}

\noindent The minimum weight FVS-4CL can be expressed as:

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \label{courcelle:weighted}
    \min_{F \subseteq V} & \ w(F) : |F| \leq k \\
    & \land \forall v u w z: \\
    & v \in (V \backslash F) \land u \in (V \backslash F) \land w \in (V \backslash F) \land z \in (V \backslash F) \\
    & \land v \neq w \land u \neq z \\
    & \land (v, u) \in E \land (u, w) \in E \land (w, z) \in E \land (v, z) \in E \to \bot
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsubsection{Exact algorithm for weighted FVS-$\rho$CL using MSOL}

\noindent The minimum weight FVS-$\rho$CL can be expressed as:

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \label{courcelle:rho}
    \min_{F \subseteq V} & \ w(F) : |F| \leq k \\
    & \land \forall v_1 v_2 \dots v_{\rho}: \\
    & v_1 \in (V \backslash F) \land v_2 \in (V \backslash F) \dots \land v_{\rho} \in (V \backslash F) \\
    & \land (v_1 \neq v_2 \land v_1 \neq v_3 \land \dots \land v_1 \neq v_{\rho}) \\ 
    & \land (v_2 \neq v_1 \land v_2 \neq v_3 \land \dots \land v_2 \neq v_{\rho}) \\
    & \dots \\
    & \land (v_{\rho} \neq v_1 \land v_{\rho} \neq v_2 \land \dots \land v_{\rho} \neq v_{\rho-1}) \\
    & \land (v_1, v_2) \in E \land (v_2, v_3) \in E \land \dots \land (v_{\rho-1},v_{\rho}) \in E \land (v_{\rho}, v_1) \in E \to \bot
\end{aligned}
\]

\refstepcounter{definition}
\begin{mydefinition}[Courcelle's theorem \cite{courcelle1990monadic}]{Theorem}
    \label{theorem:courcelle}

    Assume that $\phi$ is a MSOL formula and $G$ is an n-vertex graph, and there is an evaluation 
    of all of the free variables of $\phi$. Suppose, that a tree decomposition of $G$ of width $t$ 
    is provided. Then there exists an algorithm that verifies that $\phi$ is satisfied 
    in $G$ in time $f(||\phi||, t) \cdot n$, for some computable function $f$. 
    
\end{mydefinition}
